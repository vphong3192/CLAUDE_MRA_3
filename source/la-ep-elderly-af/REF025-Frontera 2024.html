ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND Enhanced characterization of the atrial electrical substrate may lead to better comprehension of atrial
ﬁbrillation (AF) pathophysiology.
OBJECTIVE With the use of high-density substrate mapping, we sought to investigate the occurrence of
functional
electrophysiological phenomena in the left atrium and to assess potential association with arrhythmia recurrences after catheter
ablation.
METHODS Sixty-three consecutive patients with AF referred for ablation were enrolled. Analysis of conduction abnormalities
relied on two acquired left atrial electroanatomic maps (sinus and atrial paced rhythm). We classiﬁed conduction abnormalities
as ﬁxed (if these were present in both rhythms) or functional rhythm dependent (if unmasked in one of the two rhythms).
Esophagus and aorta locations were recorded to check the correspondence with abnormal conduction sites.
RESULTS There were 234 conduction abnormalities detected, of which 125 (53.4%) were functional rhythm dependent. The most
frequent anatomic site of functional phenomena was the anterior wall, followed by the posterior wall, in sinus rhythm and the pul-
monary venous antra in paced rhythm. Sites of functional phenomena in 82.6% of cases corresponded with extracardiac structures,
such as sinus of Valsalva of ascending aorta anteriorly and the esophagus posteriorly. Most (88%) areas with functional phenomena
had normal bipolar voltage. After pulmonary vein ablation, the number of residual functional phenomena is an independent
predictor of AF recurrence (hazard ratio, 2.539 [1.458–4.420]; P 5 .001) with a risk of recurrences at multivariable Cox analysis.
CONCLUSION Dual high-density mapping (during sinus and paced rhythms) is able to unmask functional, rhythm-dependent
phenomena that are predictive of AF recurrences during follow-up.

AF substrate is, at least in part, dynamic, with rate-

so-called functional phenomena, which have already been
described in the setting of ventricular tachycardia ablation12

functional phenomena than the ventricle, leading to complex
electric wavefront dynamics during AF. We therefore sought
to introduce a novel approach, called functional electrical
substrate mapping, to enhance substrate characterization in
a reliable and reproducible manner. This approach consists
of simultaneous high-density mapping of the atrial electrical
substrate in sinus rhythm with an extra atrial stimulus delivered
from the distal coronary sinus. This strategy may have poten-
tial to better identify regional electrophysiological properties
important to AF arrhythmogenesis.

Study design and enrollment

were prospectively enrolled for the purpose of this study. In-

Electrophysiological study and ablation procedure
Every patient signed the informed consent. Patients with PsAF
were cardioverted ahead of the electrophysiological procedure.
A ﬁxed or deﬂectable quadripolar or decapolar catheter (Viking;
Boston Scientiﬁc, Marlborough, MA) was introduced up to the
distal portion of the coronary sinus through the right femoral
vein. The left atrium was accessed anterogradely through a
transseptal puncture, guided by transesophageal echocardiog-
raphy. A weight-adjusted bolus of heparin (100 IU/kg) followed
by continuous intravenous infusion was administered to achieve
an activated clotting time above 280 seconds. Three-
dimensional superhigh-density mapping was ensured with Pen-
taray (spacing: 2-6-2 mm) or Octaray (spacing: 2-2-2-2-2 mm)
catheters on the CARTO system (Biosense Webster, Irvine,
CA). The parallel mapping module featured the simultaneous
acquisition of 2 distinct activation maps: the ﬁrst one during sinus
rhythm, and the second one built on the basis of a sensed extra-
stimulus delivered from the distal coronary sinus with a coupling
interval of 130 ms greater than the local effective refractory
period calculated with sensed extrastimulus (Figure 1). Ultrahigh
density was ensured, with at least 3000 homogeneously distrib-
uted electrograms (EGMs) per map (sinus and paced) with
lowest color threshold set at 5. We segmented the regions of
the left atrium as follows: anterior wall, septum, roof, posterior
wall, and pulmonary venous antra (see Supplemental ﬁle).
The site of transseptal puncture was recorded on each elec-
troanatomic map and excluded by the analysis. We recorded
the location of the ascending aorta and the esophagus with pre-
procedural computed tomography scans that were merged
during the case with the electroanatomic maps. The use of an
esophageal temperature probe (Sensitherm, FIAB, Italy) pro-
vided a further check for the intraprocedural correlation of
esophageal location and the merged computed tomography
scan/electroanatomic maps (to exclude esophagus dislocation).
Propagation pattern and EGM analysis
Three-dimensional maps were reviewed in detail by authors
F.V. and F.C. In case of discrepancy, a third reviewer (A.F.),
blinded to prior analysis, was consulted. The 2 propagations
(sinus rhythm vs atrial extrastimulus paced rhythm) were
analyzed with isochrones to search for areas of abnormal
conduction (settings for isochrones and 3D mapping system
(CARTO): see Supplemental ﬁle). The corresponding bipolar
atrial EGMs (ﬁltered at 30–300 Hz) were considered for anal-
ysis. EGMs were analyzed in terms of duration and ampli-
tude. Fractionated atrial signals were deﬁned as EGMs with
>3 deﬂections, and the local conduction velocity for each
site was calculated according to previously established
methodology.13 The conduction velocities were calculated
on the CARTO 3-dimensional mapping system from the
difference in timing and the known distance between
recording points of the entrance and exit of the slow conduc-
tion area.

Electrophysiological phenomena
Normal activation was deﬁned as a homogeneous traveling
wavefront with a linear spreading pattern in the principal di-
rection of propagation. Abnormal sites of activation were
deﬁned as those exhibiting a change in propagation pattern
and were classiﬁed as follows:
(cid:1) pivot sites, deﬁned as discrete zones where the wavefront
makes a clockwise or counterclockwise turn on the activa-
tion map, proceeding in a different direction (with an angle
>45 degrees); and
(cid:1) corridors of slow conduction, deﬁned as regions with >3
isochrones within a 1-cm radius.
Identiﬁcation of sites of abnormal conduction was per-
formed in each of the 2 rhythms. For each identiﬁed site, 3 be-
haviors were considered: (A) abnormal conduction occurring
only during the sinus rhythm; (B) abnormal conduction occur-
ring only during atrial extrastimulus paced rhythm; (C) pres-
ence of the abnormal conduction in both electroanatomic
activation maps. On the basis of this classiﬁcation, we distin-
guished electrophysiological phenomena as being functional
rhythm-dependent (A or B) or ﬁxed to the substrate (C).
Voltage amplitude at each abnormal conduction site was
collected. Non diseased areas were those with bipolar
voltage threshold >0.5 mV.
Ablation
Radiofrequency was the only energy source used. After the
identiﬁcation of rhythm-dependent conduction abnormal-
ities, standard wide antral circumferential ablation of the pul-
monary vein (PV) was accomplished in every patient. The
analysis of
functional conduction abnormalities was per-
formed online, during the procedure, and if these were close
to the designated line of ablation, the operator included these
in the scheme of wide antral ablation. Signal analysis inside
the PVs and pacing maneuvers were carried out to ensure
entrance/exit block. Adenosine testing was performed at
the discretion of the operator. Residual functional rhythm-
dependent phenomena were considered for the analysis.
Follow-up
Antiarrhythmic drugs were discontinued within 90 days after
the ablation procedure. All patients were regularly followed
up with clinical visits and 48-hour electrocardiographic Holter
monitoring at 3, 6, and 12 months. None of the participants
missed follow-up. During intervisit intervals, where symptoms
suggestive of arrhythmias were reported, additional electro-
cardiographic or Holter monitoring was performed.
Statistical analysis
Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and per-
centages; continuous quantitative variables, as mean 6 SD
or median and interquartile range as appropriate. Differences
between groups were assessed by the Student t-test after
Levene test for equality of variance and the Mann-Whitney
U test for nonparametric variables, as appropriate. The pro-
portion of the categorical variables was compared by a c2
analysis with Yates correction if necessary. For survival anal-
ysis, log-rank Mantel-Cox test and, checking for proportional
hazards assumption, univariable Cox proportional hazards
regression models were performed; for all signiﬁcant variables
(P < .05), multivariable analysis was also performed. Two-
tailed P values <.05 were considered to indicate statistical
signiﬁcance. All statistical analyses were performed with R
software v4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing,
Vienna, Austria).

Discussion
Mapping during sinus rhythm
This research study provides new insights on the atrial
electrical substrate in patients with PAF and PsAF. The major
ﬁndings are as follows.
(cid:1) Dual high-density mapping (during sinus rhythm and atrial
sensed extrastimulus) highlights differences in areas of
conduction abnormalities represented by pivot sites and
slow conduction.
(cid:1) Change in direction and velocity of the traveling wavefront
through an extrastimulus delivered with short coupling in-
terval unmasks sites of “functional” rhythm-dependent
conduction abnormalities that are predictive of AF recur-
rences during follow-up.
(cid:1) Functional conduction abnormalities occur at sites with pre-
served voltage and are frequently linked to anatomic struc-
tures, such as the esophagus and sinus of Valsalva of the
ascending aorta.
Analysis of sinus rhythm maps provides baseline substrate
characterization in patients with AF. The number and severity
of slow conduction corridors and pivot points quantitatively
characterize AF progression from the paroxysmal to the persis-
tent form.14 These represent key players in AF, as conﬁrmed by
numerical simulations: reentrant drivers tend to anchor in re-
gions with severe conduction abnormalities, whereas they
demonstrate unstable behavior in border zones.15
Relying solely on sinus rhythm maps, however, can lead to
the omission of rate-dependent or directionality-dependent
phenomena that also contribute to the maintenance of AF.
To overcome this, we have designed a mapping protocol
that can be added to routine AF ablation workﬂows and is
easily reproducible in any electrophysiological
laboratory
setting. The protocol enables the analysis of variable re-
sponses of left atrial conduction to changes in the direction
and velocity of the traveling wavefront.

It is widely recognized that the direction of the wavefront
and the coupling interval of extrastimulus are crucial factors
in the formation of complex EGMs, which help reveal dispar-
ities in propagation velocity between neighboring ﬁbers.16
Structural anisotropy of cardiac tissue signiﬁcantly inﬂuences
direction-dependent conduction disparities.17,18 Physiologic
direction-dependent conduction characteristics become
more pronounced with chronic left atrial stretch secondary
to long-standing volume or pressure overload19 and mark
an initial step in the AF electrical and structural remodeling
process.
Rate dependency has been demonstrated in a previous
study by Huang and coworkers20 to be useful in unmasking
lines of block between the coronary sinus and the left atrium,
depending on coronary sinus pacing frequency. In 268 pa-
tients with and without a history of AF, Mouws and col-
leagues21 showed that patients with AF more often present
with continuous lines of conduction block located at the PV
area. Wong and colleagues19 demonstrated that direction-
dependent conduction may explain the propensity for devel-
opment of AF when ectopic triggers from the left atrium
initiate differing conduction wavefronts.
In this research study, we illustrate that activating the left
atrium with 2 different wavefront directions and at 2 different
cycle lengths unveils functional conduction abnormalities,
accounting for the 53.4% of all conduction disturbances
observed in this population. This study also shows that these
functional
are more
rhythm-dependent phenomena
frequently observed in patients with PsAF. The emergence
of these ﬁndings raises the question of whether the func-
tional conduction abnormalities will transition into structural
phenomena as the disease progresses. We acknowledge
that performing this functional mapping to the atria of
healthy individuals could provide valuable insights into the
characterization of the electrical atrial substrate. By charac-
terizing the electrical remodeling process in its earliest
stages, this approach may help delineate the transition
from functional to structural abnormalities, offering a clearer
understanding of the pathophysiologic mechanisms under-
lying AF progression.

PVI remains the primary therapeutic approach for AF. Howev-
er, this procedure does not encompass the entirety of the
complex pathogenesis of AF. PVI demonstrates success rates
ranging from 50% to 70% in patients with PsAF, and a signif-
icant proportion of patients have recurrent AF episodes.
Consequently, the current therapeutic options for such pa-
tients remain limited. This research study paves the way for
a new methodology to characterize the atrial substrate in pa-
tients with AF. The protocol of mapping during sinus rhythm
and with an extrastimulus delivered from the coronary sinus
is easily reproducible and reliable in every electrophysiology
laboratory. The sites of slow conduction and pivot sites iden-
tiﬁed during sinus rhythm or paced rhythm may better charac-
terize
circuit
sustainment.14 In our research study, we proved that most
functional conduction abnormalities unmasked by atrial
paced extrastimulus were at the posterior wall and at the PV
antra. Slow conduction corridors are potentially able to
generate pivot sites, which in turn may provide anchoring
sites for reentrant drivers.15 Hence, we hypothesize that by
including these corridors in the ablation lesion set, we would
ensure additional drivers (and not just those within the PVs) to
be eliminated. This can then translate to improved freedom
from AF. The ablation of these areas may represent a new
frontier in the treatment of AF, and our preliminary data
demonstrated not only that the unmasking of functional con-
duction abnormalities can be reproducibly achievable in any
laboratory setting, but also that the number of residual func-
tional phenomena is predictive of AF recurrences as
conﬁrmed in univariable and multivariable cox proportional
hazards models. This would suggest that these areas might
be directly involved in initiating AF circuits and that their abla-
tion may signiﬁcantly reduce the risk of recurrence. Further
studies and research are needed to explore the role of these
electrophysiological phenomena in patients with AF and to
prove that the functional slow conduction ablation is a new
possible target in AF ablation.

We also acknowledge that the recurrences of AF could
have been driven by PV reconnection, and hence future
studies with electroanatomic mapping in recurrences as well
as a comparison of the evoked response with different pacing
sites would be useful. Another limitation of this research is that
functional mapping could not be pursued in every patient
because induction of AF can occur during the mapping pro-
cedure. This AF induction can be caused either by the extra
beat stimulation protocol or, in most cases, by the manipula-
tion of the mapping catheter in the left atrium.
Conclusion
Functional substrate mapping can be performed using spe-
ciﬁc workﬂow that can be reproducible in every electrophys-
iology laboratory, and this may represent a novel method to
deﬁne the dynamic electrical substrate in patients undergo-
ing AF ablation procedures. Dual high-density mapping dur-
ing sinus and paced rhythms is able to unmask functional,
rhythm-dependent phenomena that are independent pre-
dictors of AF recurrences during the follow-up. Such sub-
strate characterization can lead to personalized treatment
strategies that are tailored to the individual characteristics
of patients with AF.
