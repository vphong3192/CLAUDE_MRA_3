Abstract

The incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF), the most common sustained arrhythmia and a major

public health burden, increases exponentially with age. However, mechanisms underlying

MANUSCRIPT

ACCEPTED

this long-recognized association remain incompletely understood. Experimental and human

studies have demonstrated the involvement of aging in several arrhythmogenic processes,

including atrial electrical and structural remodeling, disturbed calcium homeostasis, and

enhanced atrial ectopic activity/increased vulnerability to reentry-induction. Given this wide

range of putative mechanisms, the task of delineating the specific effects of aging responsible

for AF promotion is not simple, as aging is itself associated with increasing prevalence of a

host of AF-predisposing conditions, including heart failure, coronary artery disease and

hypertension. While we usually think of old age promoting AF, there is also evidence that

young age may actually have a protective effect against AF occurrence. For example, the low

AF-incidence among populations of young patients with significant structural congenital

heart disease and substantial atrial enlargement/remodeling suggests that younger age might

protect against fibrillation in the diseased atrium: efforts at understating how younger age

may prevent AF might be helpful in elucidating missing mechanistic links between AF and

age. The goal of the present paper is to review the epidemiological and pathophysiological

evidence regarding mechanisms underlying age-related AF. While the therapeutic options for

AF have recently improved, major gaps still remain and a better understanding of the special

relationship between age and AF may be important for the identification of new targets for

therapeutic innovation.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a global public-health challenge, with increased mortality and major

MANUSCRIPT

ACCEPTED

morbidity including stroke, heart failure (HF) and cognitive decline1–3. AF is a multifactorial

and heterogeneous disease, for which underlying mechanisms have been studied

extensively4–8. While aging is the strongest independent AF risk factor, the mechanisms

underlying aging-related AF promotion remain poorly understood9–11. Furthermore, it is even

less well-understood why AF is the least common atrial arrhythmia under age 50 and is rare

in younger patients with enlarged atria and extensive atrial remodeling, among whom

organized atrial tachyarrhythmias are common4,12,13. These observations suggest that age may

have a two-sided role in governing AF likelihood (Figure 1), both increasing AF-risk with

aging and reducing AF-risk in the young, with the latter component not to our knowledge

widely appreciated. The goal of this paper is to examine the evidence that supports the role of

age as an AF-determinant and to discuss the potential mechanisms and translational

significance.

Population-aging accounts for increasing AF prevalence

Estimating AF prevalence is challenging, considering the many patients with silent AF,

representing up to 35% of AF-patients in some series4. Recent studies using tools to capture

patients with silent or paroxysmal AF estimate that the number of individuals with AF in the

United States will increase from 5.6 (at present) to 15.9 million in 205014–17. Part of this

increase is related to a decrease in AF-related mortality (3-year mortality rate decreasing

from 45% to 42% from 1993 to 2005) associated with improved diagnostic and therapeutic

strategies, along with better detection of cases with less severe underlying disease18.

However, the age-dependence of AF, combined with population aging, suggests that aging is

the main contributor to the expected 2.5-fold increase in AF incidence15,16,19. The association

of aging with AF has been well-established by multiple epidemiological studies9,15,16,20,21.

The increase in incident AF is particularly steep between over age 6018. AF-prevalence

ACCEPTED

exceeds 20% in the ≥80-year old population, and AF-incidence progressively doubles with

each decade after 654. This prevalence may be underestimated as more elderly patients have

asymptomatic AF22.

Age influences AF clinical profile and progression

While recent epidemiological studies show that the AF-incidence is increasing in younger

individuals23, AF in this population is often associated with underlying cardiac disease,

genetic factors and/or predisposing conditions like obesity, structural or congenital heart

disease, and intensive sports-activity24,25. In a recent retrospective study of younger patients

with AF (mean age 46), only 11% of patients were free of AF risk factors or comorbidities,

whereas 44% had hypertension and 25% had a familial history of AF24.

AF progression from paroxysmal to permanent forms appears to be less common in

young vs. older patients. In a retrospective study of 468 younger patients (mean age 46) with

a median follow-up of 7.2 years, the yearly AF progression-rate was 2%24. Another study

including 242 patients (mean age 43) with recently-diagnosed AF reported a similar

progression rate over a 12.1 year follow-up26. Several observational studies have identified

aging as a strong independent predictor of AF progression to non-paroxymal forms, with an

overall progression rate of about 5%27. Persistent or permanent forms of AF are reported

more frequently in older individuals28. Thus, age might be a determinant of AF-progression

from subclinical episodes to clinically-significant prevalent AF.

Aging and predisposing conditions

AF in the presence of underlying heart disease has classically been opposed to AF as a

primary electrical disorder (“lone AF”), but recent identification of AF-promoting subclinical

cardiac remodeling caused by risk factors like obesity, metabolic syndrome and intensive

MANUSCRIPT

ACCEPTED

physical training have complicated this distinction and greatly reduced the number of cases

without any potential underlying cause4,29. The abnormal atrial substrate that maintains AF

often results from interactions between risk factors and predisposing conditions. Many

important risk factors become increasingly prevalent with aging, and are major contributors

to the role of aging in AF-development; their contribution needs to be distinguished from that

of age per se.

Hypertension accounts for about 14% of incident AF4. In the Framingham study,

hypertension conferred a 1.5-fold independent risk of new-onset AF14. The contribution of

hypertension to incident AF increases with age14. While hypertension is itself strongly

associated with aging, epidemiological data suggest that hypertension-related AF is not

strictly proportional to the prevalence of hypertension, suggesting an interaction of

hypertension with aging in AF-promotion14,30. The mechanisms underlying hypertension-

related AF-risk have been investigated in several experimental studies and include atrial

enlargement, fibrosis, and conduction abnormalities, reduced connexin-43 expression,

variable changes in electrophysiological properties of the atria, and cellular Ca2+-

dysregulation31–36. The hypertension-related AF-substrate is not specific and shares many

similarities with that associated with aging; thus, aging likely plays an important role in

facilitating electrical and structural alterations promoted by hypertension.

AF and HF have a special relationship. They share risk factors and epidemiological

similarities and they predispose to one another37. In the Framingham study, 41% of

individuals with HF had prevalent AF, while 42% of subjects with AF had experienced a

prior episode of HF17. In a population-based Scottish study, AF was associated with a 3-fold

increased risk of incident HF38. Both AF and HF are linked to structural,

electrophysiological, and neurohormonal alterations that lead to their mutual evolution and

perpetuation39. Aging-related cardiac remodeling shares mechanisms with HF including

fibrosis, activation of signaling pathways promoting spontaneous intracellular Ca2+-releases

and dysfunction of cardiac ryanodine-receptors (RyR2)40,41. In a canine HF-model with HF

induced by rapid ventricular pacing, the ability to maintain AF was greatly increased and

histological examination showed extensive atrial fibrosis42. In pulmonary vein (PV)

cardiomyocytes of HF-rabbits, late Na+ (INaL), Na+/Ca2+-exchanger and transient-inward

currents were increased, and L-type calcium current (ICaL) was decreased43.

There is experimental evidence supporting the concept of an “aging cardiomyopathy”,

characterized by decreased contractile function and impaired relaxation along with

pathological compensatory mechanisms like hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis and electrical

remodeling41,44,45. Aging-related cardiomyopathy could provide a substrate for HF at the

ventricular level and AF at the atrial level, with each contributing to the progression of the

other. Aging-related cardiomyopathy conspires with cardiac diseases that become more

prevalent with aging to produce AF-promotion that accelerates progressively with aging.

Pathophysiology of aging-related AF

Numerous experimental and clinical studies have investigated the pathophysiological links

between AF and aging, which are illustrated schematically in Figure 2. Age-related atrial

abnormalities that might increase AF-vulnerability include changes in ion-channel function

Structural remodeling and conduction abnormalities

Aging is associated with significant atrial structural changes (Table 1A), particularly atrial

fibrosis, which plays a major role in the AF-substrate46. LA size, a major biomarker of AF

and a determinant of AF perpetuation, increases with age in humans47. Atrial fibrosis

underlies atrial conduction abnormalities that play a central role in spatially anchoring

reentrant AF drivers46,48–50. The extent of low-voltage zones on electroanatomical monitoring,

often identified with fibrosis, is strongly associated with patient age51. In a canine model,

aged atria have a 3-fold greater density of fibrous tissue, with a ubiquitous distribution in

both atria52; however, in a rat study, aging-related fibrosis was spatially-heterogeneous53.

Human histopathological studies from patients undergoing cardiac surgery, with54 or

without55,56 a history of AF show an association between age and atrial fibrosis. Aged mice

show increased atrial fibrous tissue, accompanied by conduction abnormalities and increased

AF-vulnerability57. A major aspect of age-related fibrosis is its non-uniform anisotropic

distribution, which preferentially affects lateral rather than longitudinal conduction,

promoting slower and zig-zag propagation that increases the likelihood of reentry-initiation

by premature activations58. Atrial microfibrosis favours reentrant activity even in the absence

of underlying repolarization dispersion, since fast rates can dynamically create such

heterogeneities59, and through source/sink mismatches can initiate reentry despite otherwise

normal electrophysiological properties60. Spatial heterogeneity of atrial fibrosis also occurs at

a macroscopic level. A human histopathologic analysis and more recently a magnetic

resonance imaging study showed that aging-related atrial fibrosis preferentially affects the

8

left-atrial posterior wall, consistent with a recognized central role for this region in AF-

maintenance61,62.

In addition to direct effects on conduction, atrial fibrosis might indirectly influence

cardiac electrical activity and the consequences of electrical remodeling. Fibroblasts have the

MANUSCRIPT

ACCEPTED

capacity to couple to cardiomyocytes, providing an electrical load with the potential to alter

AP-properties and lead to both automatic and reentrant arrhythmic activity63–65. The extent of

fibroblast-to-cardiomyocyte coupling remains uncertain, but if it is confirmed to be

significant the effects of fibroblasts on coupled cardiomyocytes in fibrotic areas could

contribute importantly to AF-promotion.

Evidence for a clinically-relevant contribution of fibrosis to aging-related AF comes

from observations regarding the relationships between atrial fibrosis and AF pattern. There is

extensive evidence that fibrosis is involved in the substrate for persistent AF46,50, and atrial

fibrosis has recently been associated with AF-recurrence after catheter ablation49. The age-

related predominance of persistent AF-forms and the higher AF recurrence-rate after catheter

ablation in older individuals might thus be related to a greater extent of atrial fibrosis66.

In addition to fibrosis, a variety of non-fibrotic structural alterations are also

candidates to participate in aging-related AF-promotion. Senile amyloidosis, best known to

be a cause of HF with preserved ejection fraction among the elderly, is another aspect of

aging-related atrial structural remodeling. Amyloid deposits affect all cardiac chambers,

particularly the atria, and atrial amyloidosis has been implicated as a potential AF substrate

even in the absence of clinical amyloid cardiomyopathy67. Cellular aging is associated with

ultrastructural remodeling including apoptosis, autophagy and compensatory hypertrophy68.

Increased autophagy appears to contribute to the AF-maintaining substrate69. Atrial

cardiomyocyte hypertrophy alters cellular Ca2+-transients in a way that may promote AF70.

Aging-related atrial conduction disturbances that correlate with structural remodeling

are also linked to alterations in membrane protein expression. In a canine optical mapping

study, the expression of connexin-43, which forms cell-to-cell gap junctions, was reduced

and redistributed to lateral margins, thus specifically affecting transverse conduction71. Atrial

MANUSCRIPT

ACCEPTED

connexin-43 downregulation in hearts of older humans and rabbits has been associated with

c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)-activation, promoting reentry and AF in aging hearts72.

Electroanatomical studies in humans have shown an association between age and the

presence of areas of low voltage and slowed conduction, characterized by fractionated

electrograms36,73,74. These areas, which co-localize with atrial fibrosis identified by magnetic

resonance imaging, are thought to play a pivotal role in non-paroxysmal AF pathophysiology

and are major targets of AF catheter ablation75,76.

In summary, aging impairs atrial conduction through multiple mechanisms including

tissue-fibrosis, amyloid deposition and connexin-dysregulation, increasing both the

vulnerability to reentry-induction and its sustainability once induced.

Electrical remodeling

There have been many studies exploring aging-related electrophysiological changes in the

atria, with somewhat discordant findings (Table 1B). In animal models, conventional

microelectrode recording and patch-clamp studies have reproducibly shown AP-duration

(APD) prolongation and resting membrane potential depolarization, both phenomena

favoring afterdepolarizations and arrhythmogenesis77–80. Most importantly, these changes in

AP-contour are spatially heterogeneous, causing regional dispersion of atrial effective

refractory period (ERP)80, which promotes reentry formation and maintenance. In a rat study,

APD and ERP were prolonged in aged right atria but significantly shortened in left atria79.

Aging was associated with a markedly increased APD in rabbit left-atrial posterior walls, a

afterdepolarizations (DADs)81. Dispersion of refractoriness is an important potential

promoter of AF-vulnerability82. In dogs, spatial dispersion of repolarization potentiates the

arrhythmogenicity of fast rates and is central for the perpetuation of vagally-mediated AF83,84.

In humans, inhomogeneity of atrial refractoriness has been implicated in paroxysmal and

chronic AF85. While some human electrophysiological analyses showed increased atrial ERP

with aging86,87, others showed no effect of age on atrial refractoriness, but with important

limitations including recording from a single site, analyses on individuals taking anti-

arrhythmic drugs and a variable definition of aging with few subjects over 7088–90.

A range of atrial ion-channels have been studied in animal models91. Upregulated

channels include those carrying the atrial-specific ultrarapid delayed-rectifier current IKur, the

transient outward (Ito) and the sustained (Isus) K+-currents92. The acetylcholine-induced

potassium current (IKACh), which mediates parasympathetic responses, is more strongly-

expressed in aged atria78. Reduced currents in aged atria include ICaL

The many ion-channel changes that have been reported with aging provide potential

mechanisms for AF-promoting electrophysiological remodeling, but the variability among

studies makes the precise role of any specific change uncertain. Nevertheless, AP-studies

generally show resting-potential depolarization and APD-prolongation, changes that favor the

development of arrhythmogenic afterdepolarizations.

Sinus-node dysfunction

The association of sinus node dysfunction (SND) with aging is well-known and its incidence

parallels that of AF94. Animal studies have identified age-related alterations in sinus-node

pacemaker properties, including decreases in INa, in connexin-43 expression and in L-type

calcium channel activity95,96. Patients with SND have evidence of diffuse atrial

cardiomyopathy including atrial fibrosis, increased right-atrial refractoriness and conduction

slowing97. Patients with AF and SND display more extensive low-voltage areas with

fragmented electrograms than patients with AF and no SND98. Both SND and AF may thus

ACCEPTED

result from a common underlying atrial cardiomyopathy related to aging. Often, AF precedes

manifest SND, advancing the concept of a continuum in atrial remodeling from AF to SND99.

It is also possible that SND increases the risk of AF by promoting rhythm irregularities that

increase atrial ERP dispersion and ectopic complex emergence. Whether the close association

between SND and AF as a function of age reflects solely a common set of aging-induced

mechanisms, or whether there are causal links between the two, remains to be definitively

determined.

Ca2+-handling and ectopic activity

Cardiomyocyte Ca2+-handling abnormalities play a major role in the pathophysiology of

arrhythmias, particularly AF100. Experimental studies closely link age-related Ca2+-handling

abnormalities to DAD-formation and associated spontaneous atrial ectopic activity (Table

1C), which plays a central role in AF initiation5,101. Increased spontaneous diastolic Ca2+-

release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) through RyR2-leak has been found in atrial

samples from elderly patients102,103, along with phospholamban hyperphosphorylation104,

which promotes SR Ca2+-loading by disinhibiting the Ca2+-uptake protein SR Ca2+-ATPase

(SERCA). Aging has been associated with an increase in diastolic intracellular Ca2+ at the

PV-left atrial junction, leading to triggered activity and AF105. Recently, in a mouse model,

Yan et al. unraveled a potential molecular mechanism for aging-related AF promotion

through Ca2+-handling dysregulation103. Their experiments demonstrated an increase in JNK-

activation in aged mouse and human atrial samples, causing diastolic SR Ca2+-leak,

Other mechanisms might also contribute to the increased atrial triggered activity

associated with aging. Resting membrane potential depolarization in aged rabbit left-atrial

posterior walls is associated with a greater propensity for DAD-generation81, which may be

enhanced SR diastolic Ca2+-leak, supporting a role of RyR2-dysfunction in age-related PV

Clinical data support a role for atrial ectopy in age-related AF risk. Older individuals

have a greater burden of atrial premature beats112, which has been associated with a greater

recurrence-rate of paroxysmal AF in older individuals following anti-arrhythmic therapy or

catheter ablation113. PVs are larger in older adults47, and their size decreases after successful

catheter ablation114. In a human electroanatomical study, aging was associated with a

significant reduction in PV voltage, conduction slowing, and electrogram fractionation,

suggesting that aging-related atrial structural remodeling may also affect AF triggers such as

PV arrhythmogenesis115.

Possible role of sex hormones

Testosterone levels, which decrease with aging, have been associated with incident AF in

several studies116,117. Castration of rats increases AF inducibility, an effect associated with

RyR2-overexpression and reversed by testosterone-administration118. Androgen-receptor

knockout mice show APD-prolongation and decreased resting membrane potential, related to

reduced expression of inward-rectifier K+-channels119. In another study, INaL was enhanced in

castrated mice, and INaL inhibitors prevented AF120. These studies point to an

afterdepolarization-promoting effect of decreasing testosterone-activity with aging; however,

further work is needed to clarify the precise role in AF, as well as the contribution of other

hormonal changes.

Age and AF: A two-sided relationship?

The increased propensity for AF in the elderly is well recognized, but the complementary

issue of whether young age per se protects against AF has not been extensively considered.

The fact that AF remains rare in young patients with substantial atrial remodeling argues in

favor of this idea. A striking example comes from patients with complex congenital heart

disease (CHD) with severely enlarged atria, extensive atrial fibrosis and surgical scars, in

whom organized atrial tachyarrhythmias are common but AF-prevalence remains low12. A

potential conceptual model of age-related AF-occurrence is shown in Figure 1. A variety of

stressors cause atrial remodeling and promote AF. While many of these (e.g. hypertension,

more likely. However, the occurrence of AF is also dependent on aging-induced atrial

Experimental evidence bearing on AF-susceptibility in the young

There is very limited experimental evidence regarding a potential protective effect of youth

against AF. In a study comparing 22-to-24 month-old to 2-3 month-old rats, equivalent to

about 60- versus 15-year old humans, Hayashi and colleagues showed that cell-to-cell

uncoupling by heptanol induced AF in old atria but provoked sustained regular atrial

tachyarrhythmia in young atria53. Older atria had much greater interstitial fibrous-tissue

content. More work is clearly needed to evaluate the mechanistic basis for the resistance

against AF in younger individuals.

Observations in athletes and adults with congenital heart disease

High-intensity physical activity (defined by sweating and deep-breathing) with a total

lifetime accumulation of over 2000 hours is associated with an increased risk of AF (odds-

ratio 3.88 versus sedentary), whereas fewer accumulated hours of high-intensity activity are

protective (odds ratio 0.38 versus sedentary)121. AF-prevalence among competitive athletes

ranges from 0.2% to 16.7%, conferring a 8.8 to 14.4-fold greater risk of AF compared to non-

athletic controls122. Potential underlying mechanisms include atrial remodeling and fibrosis

through volume overload, elevated blood pressure during exercise with transient atrial stretch

association between endurance sports and AF-risk is closely linked to age. While AF-

prevalence in very young athletes is low, it increases significantly in the adult athlete, in a

fashion that is independent of the intensity level. AF prevalence was 0.2% in a large

population of 1,777 young competitive athletes (mean age 24±6 years)124. In contrast,

middle-aged or veteran athletes have a significant excess in AF-prevalence compared to age-

middle-aged athletes is well recognized125, and suggests a protective effect of younger age as

an explanation of the age-dependence of endurance sport-related AF.

In the adult CHD population, which is expanding and aging127, atrial arrhythmias are

the most common complication and the leading cause of morbidity and hospitalizations4.

Their incidence dramatically increases by the age of 30 and 50% of adult CHD patients will

experience atrial arrhythmia during their lifetime128. In a multicenter study of 482 CHD

patients with atrial arrhythmias, intra-atrial reentrant tachycardia accounted for over 60%12,

related to the presence of surgical scars that provide anatomical barriers for macroreentry. In

a single-center retrospective study, while only 8.3% of CHD patients had experienced AF by

age 40, 53.7% of patients over 50 developed AF129. In a more recent multicenter study, AF

accounted for 9.3% of atrial arrhythmias in patients under 20 and became the most common

arrhythmia in patients over 50, representing over 50% of atrial arrhythmias12.

In a multicenter study of 556 patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot, there was a

steep increase in AF-prevalence in the fifth decade of life130. The prevalence-curve for AF

paralleled that of the general population, albeit with an approximate 20-year leftward shift in

time-dependency. This observation supports the notion that there is a continuous effect of age

on AF-risk that is not restricted to the elderly. Indeed, despite the younger age of this cohort

Conclusions

The available evidence supports the concept of an «aging-related atrial cardiomyopathy»

resulting from the multiple structural, electrophysiological, ionic and molecular changes

related both to aging itself and to aging-related comorbidities and cardiovascular risk factors.

Age appears to be a continuous determinant of AF-risk: old age promotes AF, but young age

appears to protect against AF-promoting stressors and conditions. The molecular mechanisms

underlying this relationship remain incompletely understood; their better characterization

might provide new insights and innovative targets for AF treatment.

Figure 1. Age as a two-sided atrial fibrillation (AF) determinant. A conceptual model for

the age-related factors governing AF prevalence. Aging produces a cardiomyopathy, the

intensity of which increases with advancing years as shown in red. On the other hand,

resilience against AF-promoting stressors is greatest early in life, and wanes as time goes on

as shown in blue. In the general population (dark blue curve), the incidence of AF is

dominated by the development of aging-related myopathy. Stressors like hypertension, heart

failure, valve disease, coronary artery disease, obesity, congenital heart diseases and intensive

physical training promote the development of AF. With strong early stressors like complex

congenital heart disease, the AF prevalence curve switches to the left, with the resilience

threshold reached much earlier in life.
