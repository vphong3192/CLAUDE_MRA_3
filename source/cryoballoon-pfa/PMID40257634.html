Abstract

Background Pulsed eld ablation (PFA) and high-power short-duration radiofrequency ablation (HPSD) are emerging tech- niques for treating atrial brillation (AF), oering promising results compared to cryoballoon ablation (CBA). This network meta-analysis aims to evaluates the ecacy and safety of PFA, HPSD, and CBA.

Method PubMed, Scopus and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were systematically searched for relevant stud- ies until October 2024. The primary outcome is freedom from atrial arrhythmia. A random-eects model was used for data synthesis, and P-scores were employed for outcome ranking. Point estimation (odd ratios) was calculated for comparisons.

Results Eighteen studies were included in our network meta-analysis, involving 7,071 atrial brillation patients. Among them, 2,023 (29%), 3,725 (53%), and 1,323 (18%) patients underwent PFA, CBA, and HPSD, respectively. PFA demon- strated a higher freedom from atrial arrhythmia, with an odds ratio (OR) of 3.63 (95% CI: 2.95–4.46) compared to CBA and 1.89 (95% CI: 1.47–2.43) compared to HPSD. However, PFA was associated with a higher risk of complications (OR = 6.54, 95% CI: 2.13–20.00) compared to CBA, while HPSD showed an insignicant association with a lower risk of complications compared to CBA (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.15–2.42). PFA had the shortest procedural time (P-score: 100%), while HPSD had the longest (P-score: 0%). In contrast, HPSD had the shortest uoroscopic time, with P-scores of 100%, 46%, and 3% for HPSD, PFA, and CBA, respectively.

Conclusion PFA demonstrated higher ecacy but also a higher risk of complications compared to HPSD and CBA. HPSD showed greater ecacy with comparable safety to CBA.

1 Introduction

Atrial brillation (AF), the most prevalent form of cardiac arrhythmias, has alarmingly increased in incidence globally [1]. Against this backdrop, catheter ablation has evolved into a cornerstone treatment over the past several decades, adapting to the ever-changing medical landscape [ ]. More 2 recent studies have shown that ablation for AF is more eective than antiarrhythmic drugs [3].

While conventional radiofrequency ablation has been the mainstay tool for mechanically rhythm control, its limita- tions prompted the advent of High-Power Short Duration

radiofrequency ablation (HPSD) in 2006 [4]. This tech- nique uses higher power (50–70 watts) for a shorter dura- tion to create the necessary lesions for treating AF, with the potential benets of reducing procedure times and possibly

improving safety and ecacy [5]. Concurrently, Cryobal- loon ablation (CBA), emerged in 2007, has gained attention for its lower thromboembolic risks and minimal incidence of permanent atrioventricular block [ ]. 6 Pulsed Field Ablation (PFA), also known as Electropora- tion, signied a paradigm shift in cardiac electrophysiology with its non-thermal approach, mitigating the risk of col- lateral damage to cardiac tissues and neighboring structures [7]. The evolving narrative of these treatment modalities calls for a comprehensive comparative evaluation of their safety and ecacy.

In this study, we engage a network meta-analysis to tran- scend the connes of traditional meta-analyses that compare treatments in a pairwise fashion. This approach empowers us to perform both direct and indirect comparisons, thread- ing together multiple treatment arms and studies to paint

a holistic picture of AF ablation strategies (HPSD, CBA,

PFA). By dissecting the intricate web of interventional outcomes, our analysis aims to distill robust conclusions that can guide clinical decisions in the landscape of AF treatment.

2 Methods

2.1 Literature review and search strategy

This network meta-analysis protocol was performed accord- ing to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review

and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [8]. Two investi- gators (ND and AS) independently conducted the literature search from four databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane

Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) dating from the database inception to October 2024. The search encompassed specic terms, including “Atrial brillation”, “Cryoballoon ablation”, “High power short duration radio-

frequency ablation” and “Pulsed eld ablation”. The need for approval from the institutional review board was waived because publicly available cumulative published data were used.

2.2 Selection criteria

We included randomized controlled trials, case-control studies, or cohort studies that met the following inclusion

criteria: (a) the study involved patients with AF, (b) the study presented ecacy and safety outcomes of two out of

the three ablation techniques: PFA, CBA, and HPSD, and

(c) the included studies were required to report statistical outcomes in terms of the number or percentage of patients with freedom of atrial arrhythmia after undergoing ablation. Studies lacking sucient information on freedom of atrial arrhythmia counts, unpublished works, conference abstracts lacking sucient data, case reports, editorials, expert opin- ions, and review articles were excluded. Disagreements between the two investigators were resolved through dis- cussion with a third research investigator (NT).

2.3 Primary outcome

Freedom from atrial arrhythmia dened as the absence of any documented episodes of AF or atrial tachycar- dia lasting longer than 30 s, starting after a blanking period of 3 months.

2.4 Secondary outcome

● Rates of complications including death, stroke/transient ischemia attack, pulmonary vein stenosis, cardiac tam-

ponade, atrialoesophageal stula, transient/permanent phrenic nerve injury, vascular access complications. ● Procedural time. ● Fluoroscopic time.

2.6 Data synthesis and statiscal analysis

The primary outcome focused on freedom of atrial arrhyth- mia, while the secondary outcomes focused on complica-

tions, procedural time, and uoroscopic time. R software

version 4.3.3 (A Language and Environment for Statisti-

cal Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for analysis. A

frequentist Bayesian network meta-analysis model was performed using the “netmeta” package and “gemtc” pack-

age. The random-eects model was used to compile the outcomes.

The Netsplit method was employed to evaluate the con-

sistency of the model by applying the “netsplit” function. We obtained a median estimate of the odds ratio (OR) from the posterior distribution and reported it with the 2.5th to the 97.5th centiles of the distribution (95% condence interval). For continuous data, the mean dierence between treatment

modalities was calculated and reported with a 95% con- dence interval (CI).

The “netrank” function was implemented to generate the relative ranking probability of each treatment group. P-scores were reported to provide a comparative hierarchy of ecacy and safety among the treatment groups.

Sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing the

results between the frequentist network meta-analysis approach and the Bayesian network meta-analysis approach. Egger’s test and funnel plot asymmetry were used to evalu- ate publication bias.

Cohort and case-control studies were assessed for qual- ity using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which evaluates selection, comparability, and outcome through a 0–9 scor-

ing system. Studies with ≥ 7 scored as high quality, 4–6 as moderate, and < 4 as low. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were assessed with the 5-point Jadad scale, evaluat- ing randomization (2 points), blinding (2 points), and drop- out rate (1 point). Two independent reviewers (ND and AS) conducted the assessments, resolving any disagreements through discussion and consensus.

3 Results

51 studies were retrieved for a detailed full-text evaluation. Following the full-text review, 33 studies were excluded

due to irrelevant comparators (10 studies), irrelevant out- comes (15 studies), irrelevant interventions (7 studies), and duplication (1 study).

4 Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the rst network meta- analysis to compare the ecacy of PFA, HPSD, and CBA. This systematic analysis demonstrated that PFA was associ- ated with higher ecacy in achieving freedom from atrial arrhythmia compared to both CBA and HPSD. Unexpect- edly, PFA was also associated with higher complication rates compared to CBA, while there was no statistically sig- nicant dierence in complication rates between HPSD and CBA. However, procedural time and uoroscopic time were shorter with HPSD compared to both PFA and CBA.

4.1 Freedom of atrial arrhythmia

Our study demonstrated that PFA for AF was associated with a roughly 1.8- and 3.6-fold higher freedom from atrial arrhythmia when compared to HPSD and CBA, respec- tively. To elaborate, PFA exhibits higher ecacy over HPSD due to the use of microsecond-scale, high-voltage electri- cal elds that cause irreversible electroporation and desta- bilization of cell membranes—a process that contributes to cellular necrosis [ ]. Focusing on the gross lesion, the 7 radiofrequency ablation catheter creates a conglomeration of multiple circular adjacent lesions, while the PFA catheter creates homogeneous and wider lesions with a rectangular shape. These minimize the chance of myocardial injury and lead to a lower chance of arrhythmia recurrence [ ]. 27 Additionally, various parameters including ell size, shape, orientation, pulse width and amplitude, the number of pulses, monophasic or biphasic waveforms, pulse cycle duration, and the distance between the tissue and delivery electrodes have been identied to aect the transmembrane hyperpermeability.

A previous meta-analysis published in 2024 included 4,998 patients, comprising 2 RCTs and 16 observational studies, and aimed to compare PFA with thermal ablation techniques, including CBA and RFA [28]. Their outcomes showed that PFA had a signicantly lower recurrence rate compared to thermal ablation in long-term follow-up. 5 Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that PFA was associated with a higher freedom from atrial arrhythmia compared to HPSD and CBA, with shorter procedural times compared to HPSD. However, PFA was also associated with a higher risk of complications compared to the other two ablation tech-

niques. HPSD showed signicantly higher freedom from atrial arrhythmia with no dierence in safety compared to CBA.

4.3 Procedural time and uoroscopic time

According to our study, PFA and CBA procedures had sig- nicantly shorter procedural times than HPSD, with mean

dierences of approximately 44 and 31 min, respectively. Iqbal’s systematic analysis revealed that PFA has a shorter procedural time, with a mean dierence of 19.82 min com-

pared to thermal ablation techniques, including CBA and RFA [29]. Additionally, our study found that PFA and

CBA procedures had longer uoroscopic times compared

to HPSD. This can be explained by the non-uoroscopic approach used with 3D mapping and intracardiac echocar- diography in HPSD, while uoroscopy still plays an impor- tant role in PFA and CBA procedures, which are further supported by the extensive experience in using radiofre- quency ablation.

However, it is worth noting that the ablation procedures

in our study were performed by dierent operators in dif- ferent settings, which could have aected our results. PFA is also a new technology that requires a specic skill set to perform the procedure, not only for the physicians but also

for the lab sta, including technologists and scrub nurses. As experience in cardiac electrophysiology laboratories increases, the procedure and uoroscopic times may shorten in the future.

4.4 Limitation
