BACKGROUND Freedom from atrial arrhythmia (AA) recurrence 30 seconds after pulsed ﬁeld ablation (PFA) in patients with atrial ﬁbrillation (AF) was reported in PULSED AF (Pulsed Field Ablation to Irreversibly Electroporate Tissue and Treat AF; ClinialTrials.gov Identiﬁer: NCT04198701). AA burden may be a more clinically meaningful endpoint.

OBJECTIVE The purpose of this study was to determine the inﬂu- ence of monitoring strategies on AA detection and AA burden asso- ciation with quality of life (QoL) and health care utilization (HCU) after PFA.

METHODS Patients underwent 24-hour Holter monitoring at 6 and 12 months and weekly, and symptomatic transtelephonic moni- toring (TTM). AA burden post-blanking was calculated as the greater of (1) percentage of AA on total Holter time; or (2) percentage of weeks with 1 TTM with AA out of all weeks with 1 TTM.

RESULTS Freedom from all AAs varied by .20% when differing monitoring strategies were used. PFA resulted in zero burden in 69.4% of paroxysmal atrial ﬁbrillation (PAF) and 62.2% of persis-

tent atrial ﬁbrillation (PsAF) patients. Median burden was low (,9%). Most PAF and PsAF patients had 1 week of AA detection on TTM (82.6% and 75.4%) and ,30 minutes of AA per day of Holter monitoring (96.5% and 89.6%), respectively. Only PAF patients with ,10% AA burden averaged a clinically meaningful (.19 point) QoL improvement. PsAF patients experienced clinically meaningful QoL improvements irrespective of burden. Repeat ablations and car- dioversions signiﬁcantly increased with higher AA burden (P ,.01).

CONCLUSION The 30-second AA endpoint is dependent on the monitoring protocol used. PFA resulted in low AA burden for most patients, which was associated with clinically relevant improvement in QoL and reduced AA-related HCU.

Introduction Atrial ﬁbrillation (AF), the most common cardiac arrhythmia, substantially diminishes patient quality of life (QoL) and is associated with signiﬁcant health care utilization (HCU) and high economic burden.1,2 Catheter ablation is an established treatment of AF and improves QoL, reduces HCU, and lessens AA burden.3,4 However, the gold standard efﬁcacy endpoint for catheter ablation trials is based on the percentage of patients who remain free from 30 seconds of atrial arrhythmia (AA) recurrence (an episode of AF, atrial ﬂutter, or atrial tachycardia) over the follow-up period.3 This archetype deﬁnition of arrhythmia recurrence may lack clinical signiﬁcance.5,6

Secondary analysis of several clinical trials, including CAPTAF (Catheter Ablation Compared with Pharmaco- logical Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation), CIRCA-DOSE Cryoballoon vs. Irrigated Radiofrequency Catheter Abla- tion: Double Short vs. Standard Exposure Duration), CAPCOST (Prospective, Multicenter Canadian Atrial Fibrillation PVAC Cohort Study), and STAR AF II (Sub- strate and Trigger Ablation for Reduction of Atrial Fibril- lation Trial II), have suggested that AA burden is a more objective, relevant measure of clinical success for catheter ablation technologies and that a reduction in AA burden has a greater impact on QoL and HCU as opposed to solely deﬁning success by 30 seconds of AA recur- rence.2,5–7 However, the aforementioned studies used radiofrequency and/or cryoballoon ablation, and the relationship between AA burden, QoL, and HCU has not been ascertained for pulsed ﬁeld ablation (PFA). PFA is a novel ablation approach that uses high electric ﬁeld gradients to produce lesions in cardiac tissue through the mechanism of irreversible electroporation.8,9 Results of the PULSED AF trial (Pulsed Field Ablation to Irreversibly Electroporate Tissue and Treat AF) demonstrated that PFA was safe and effective in treating paroxysmal atrial ﬁbrilla- tion (PAF) and persistent atrial ﬁbrillation (PsAF). This sec- ondary analysis of the PULSED AF trial sought to assess success using different monitoring strategies, AA burden af- ter PFA, and the association between postablation AA burden, QoL and HCU.

Methods Trial design PULSED AF was a global, prospective, multicenter, non- randomized, paired single-arm trial designed to evaluate the safety and efﬁcacy of treating patients with PAF and PsAF via a PFA system (PulseSelectTM Pulsed Field Ablation Sys- tem, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). The PULSED AF trial design has been previously described in detail.10 In brief, an international steering committee (Supplemental Material) was accountable for the design, execution, study oversight, approval of data analysis and interpretation, and publication strategy. In accordance with principles deﬁned in the Declaration of Helsinki, each site obtained approval from local ethics review boards.

Study participants The patient cohort was treated at 41 centers by 67 investigators in 9 countries. Patients aged 18–80 years undergoing an index catheter ablation procedure for treatment of recurrent symp- tomatic PAF or PsAF were included. Subjects were required to have failed at least 1 class I or class III antiarrhythmic drug.3,11,12 Detailed study inclusion and exclusion criteria are deﬁned in a previous publication.10 Written informed con- sent was acquired from all patients before study enrollment.

PFA procedure The PFA procedure has been described in detail in 2 previous PULSED AF publications.10,13 In brief, clinicians executed transseptal puncture to introduce the PulseSelect catheter into the left atrium over a guidewire. The PulseSelect catheter has a circular arrangement of 9 gold, 3-mm-long electrodes through which a controlled biphasic, bipolar waveform is dispensed. The positioning of the PFA catheter’s circular array at every pulmonary vein (PV) was gauged by ﬂuoros- copy or intracardiac echocardiographic imaging. After each application (deﬁned as 4 biphasic, bipolar pulse trains lasting 100–200 ms), the catheter was repositioned to achieve full circumferential isolation of the PV. Conﬁrmation of com- plete PV isolation via entrance block after a protocol- mandated, 20-minute wait period was required.

Study follow-up Study participants were monitored for 12 months via a com- bination of transtelephonic monitoring (TTM) (weekly and symptomatic), 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) (at 3, 6, and 12 months), and 24-hour Holter monitoring (at 6 and 12 months). Arrhythmia monitoring events were adjudicated by an independent core laboratory. Patients were further monitored by virtual or in-person visits at 1 week, and 1, 3, and 12 months postablation. The AFEQT (Atrial Fibrillation Effect on QualiTy of Life) Questionnaire was used to eval- uate QoL at baseline and 12 months. AFEQT is a self- administered AF-speciﬁc health-related QoL questionnaire, with scores ranging from 0 (complete AF-related disability) to 100 (no AF-related disability). The study also collected ep- isodes of HCU including repeat ablation, cardioversion, emergency room (ER) visits, and hospitalizations.

Patients with zero burden were classiﬁed as “zero AA burden” for some analyses. Patients with any AA burden on either Holter monitoring or TTM were classiﬁed as “non–zero AA burden.” AA burden was then correlated to patient QoL and HCU.

Statistical analysis All analyses were post hoc. Continuous variables are given as mean 6 SD, and categorical variables are given as count (%). Time to AA recurrence was evaluated using analysis of variance. Accounting for variations at baseline, QoL is reported as the change from baseline to 12 months and was tested using 2-sample t tests and analysis of vari- ance. Logistic regression was used to model the effect of AA burden (treated as a continuous variable) on events of interest. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). P ,.05 was considered signiﬁcant.

Results Patient baseline characteristics The study population consisted of the 150 PAF and 150 PsAF patients from the PULSED AF trial. Both AF types were divided into zero and non–zero AA burden groups. Baseline patient characteristics are listed in Table 1.

Monitoring simulation In the PULSED AF trial, weekly and symptomatic TTMs, 3-, 6-, and 12-month 12-lead ECGs, and 6- and 12-month 24-hour Holter recordings were used to detect AA recur- rence. Under these conditions, PFA was found to be effective in 69.5% of patients with PAF and 62.3% of patients with PsAF (Monitoring Scenario A, Figure 1). To evaluate the impact of different cardiac monitoring strategies on AA detection after PFA, these results were compared to 2 other common monitoring strategies used in catheter ablation trials (Monitoring Scenarios B and C, Figure 1). With follow-up strategy outlined in Monitoring Scenario B, the estimated ef- ﬁcacy for PULSED AF increased to 70.9% for PAF and 67.8% for PsAF patients. With the follow-up strategy

outlined in Monitoring Scenario C, the success rate for PAF and PsAF patients rose to 91.3% and 86.3%, respectively.

AA burden As previously reported, full compliance with all weekly TTM was 81%. AA burden was calculated using 12,264 hours of Holter recordings in 277 patients (average 44.3 hours per patient), along with an average of 29.0 weeks with at least 1 TTM transmission for 286 patients. Zero AA burden was observed in 69.4% of the PAF and 62.2% of the PsAF cohort (Figure 2). Most patients expe- rienced ,10% burden, comprising 87.1% of the PAF and 81.8% of the PsAF group. Excluding those with zero burden, median burden was 7.1% for PAF patients and 8.7% for PsAF patients.

Distribution of time in atrial arrhythmia The distribution of the weeks and hours of AA detected via TTMs and Holter monitoring, respectively, in patients is shown in Figure 3. In most patients with AA recurrence, AA was only detected on a minority of TTMs and lasted a short duration. Overall, 82.6% of PAF and 75.4% of PsAF patients experienced 1 week of AA recurrence on TTM monitoring (Figure 3A). Similarly, 96.5% of PAF and 89.6% of PsAF pa- tients had ,30 minutes of AA detected per day of Holter monitoring (Figure 3B). More speciﬁcally, 83% of PAF and 75% of PsAF patients experienced 1 week of AA recurrence on TTM monitoring. A substantial change in QoL was signiﬁcantly associated with AA burden ,10% in the PAF subgroup, whereas all AA burden quartiles were associated with improved QoL in the PsAF group. Finally, HCU was correlated with posta- blation AA burden with a substantial increase in repeat abla- tion for AA burden .10% and cardioversion for AA burden .40%. ER visits/hospitalizations demonstrated a nonsignif- icant trend in correlation to AA burden. The adoption of the 30-second deﬁnition of AA recur- rence for catheter ablation trials was arbitrarily chosen in the original Consensus Statement for AF ablation.3 Since then, it has endured to allow consistency in reporting of clin- ical trials and because of no credible, evidence-based alterna- tive. However, the endpoint is clearly limited given that 30 seconds of AA is rarely clinically signiﬁcant to an individual patient and would not justify additional medical intervention. The endpoint is also subject to detection bias because very short episodes of AA recurrence will be missed by noncontin- uous monitoring. Intermittent monitoring has limited sensi-

tivity of 16%–71% in detecting AA recurrence of 30 seconds compared to continuous monitoring.17 In fact, suc- cess rates may vary by an absolute value of 40% in the same patient cohort depending on the monitoring strategy used.17 In the present study, the endpoint for freedom from all AAs of the PAF and PsAF groups would improve by nearly 20% and 25%, respectively, depending on the moni- toring strategy used by other ablation studies examining PFA.14,15 This result further iterates how the 30-second endpoint is highly dependent on the frequency and duration of monitoring. For instance, with the two 24-hour Holter monitoring strategy, there were only 2 time points when recurrence could have been detected compared to the up to 40 time points in the PULSED AF trial. The discordance between the 30-second recurrence endpoint and reduction in AA burden was best demonstrated by the CIRCA-DOSE study.16 Using implantable cardiac monitors (ICMs), the investigators described a 52% success rate when outcome was evaluated as freedom from 30- second AA recurrence. However, patients in that same group experienced a 99.9% reduction in total AA burden. Similarly, in the STAR AF II trial of PsAF patients, the reported freedom from AA 30 seconds was about 50%, whereas pa- tients had a 92% reduction in AA burden by a similar TTM

AA burden has not been widely adopted as an endpoint for ablation trials, principally because the degree of AA burden reduction to deﬁne success has not been well established. Clin- ically relevant AA burden reductions should correlate with meaningful improvements in patient QoL and reductions in HCU. In this study, we saw a substantial change in QoL when AA burden was reduced to ,10% in the PAF subgroup. Interestingly, all subgroups in the PsAF cohort demonstrated a substantial improvement in QoL, resulting in a nonsigniﬁcant difference in the change in QoL from baseline to 12 months among the 4 burden groups; this could be because the preabla- tion burden of these patients could have been very high (close to 100%). Therefore, even a 40% absolute reduction in the AA burden could result in meaningful improvements in QoL. A similar result was demonstrated in the STAR AF II trial in which patients with a relative reduction in AA burden even as low as 60% experienced QoL beneﬁt.7 In another study, AA burden reductions .19 hours per month were associated with an improvement in QoL.2 Finally, in the CIRCA-DOSE trial, residual AA burdens ,24 hours (or ,0.1%) were most closely associated with QoL improvement, but in a PAF popu- lation.5 Therefore, a goal of zero AA burden is not required for QoL improvement (particularly in the PsAF population), but the precise absolute or relative reduction in AA required still commands further investigation. This substudy is one of the few to correlate residual AA burden with HCU. A signiﬁcant trend in increased repeat ablation and cardioversion was demonstrated with increasing AA burden. A particular jump in the incidence of repeat abla- tion and cardioversion was seen with AA burden .10% and .40%, respectively. Interestingly, among patients with AA burden ranging from 0.01%–39.9%, a higher percentage un- derwent repeat ablation rather than cardioversion (especially in the PAF cohort). Although there was no signiﬁcant trend for AA burden and ER visits/ hospitalizations, most of the events occurred with AA burden .10%. In another study, the point determining increased HCU was AA recurrence .1 hour or total AA burden of 0.1%–1%.5 AA burden in that study was measured by an ICM and was not estimated from TTMs, resulting in differences in burden calculation based on the precision allowed by ICMs.

Study limitations First, AA burden was estimated from noncontinuous moni- toring and not on an implantable device. However, this method has beenpreviouslyvalidated,7,18and we useda veryconserva- tive approach in calculating the burden to avoid underestima- tion of the time spent with an AA. Furthermore, calculations of AA burden may overestimate actual AA burden measured from an implantable device.17 Second, we did not measure pre- ablation burden in either AF cohort. Therefore, we cannot conﬁrmthatpostablationburdenrepresentsatrueimprovement

over preablation burden. Finally, we chose to deﬁne a signiﬁ- cant improvement in QoL as .19 points for the AFEQT scale.19Other studies havereportedthata meaningfulimprove- ment can be demonstrated with as few as 5 points,20 but the higher cutoff used here is more conservative and therefore could be less likely to demonstrate a difference.

Conclusion The 30-second endpoint for AA recurrence can be substan- tially different depending on the monitoring regimen used. Most patients post-PFA had low AA burden during follow- up. AA burden was associated with QoL and an increase in HCU. In PAF patients, AA burden ,10% was associated with meaningful improvement in QoL, and AA burden .10% was associated with more repeat ablation, cardiover- sion, and possibly hospital visits.
