BACKGROUND Pulmonary-vein isolation is an effective treatment for paroxysmal atrial fibrilla- tion. Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is a nonthermal ablation method with few adverse effects beyond the myocardium. Data are lacking on outcomes after PFA as com- pared with cryoballoon ablation as assessed with continuous rhythm monitoring.

METHODS In this randomized noninferiority trial in Switzerland, we randomly assigned pa- tients with symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation in a 1:1 ratio to undergo PFA or cryoablation. All the patients received an implantable cardiac monitor to detect atrial tachyarrhythmias. The primary end point was the first recurrence of an atrial tachyarrhythmia between day 91 and day 365 after ablation. We assessed noninferi- ority using a margin of 20 percentage points for the difference in the cumulative incidence of recurrence. The safety end point was a composite of procedure-related complications.

RESULTS A total of 105 patients were assigned to undergo PFA, and 105 were assigned to undergo cryoablation. A recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia was observed between day 91 and day 365 in 39 patients in the PFA group and in 53 patients in the cryo- ablation group (Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence, 37.1% and 50.7%, respectively; between-group difference, −13.6 percentage points; 95% confidence interval, −26.9 to −0.3; P<0.001 for noninferiority, P = 0.046 for superiority). The safety end point oc- curred in 1 patient (1.0%) with PFA and in 2 patients (1.9%) with cryoablation.

CONCLUSIONS Among patients with symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, PFA was non- inferior to cryoballoon ablation with respect to the incidence of a first recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia, as assessed by continuous rhythm monitoring. atheter-based pulmonary-vein iso- lation is an effective treatment for symp- tomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and is superior to antiarrhythmic drugs in main- taining sinus rhythm and reducing symptoms.1-3 Thermal ablation by means of radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation has been the mainstay for pulmonary-vein isolation, with similar success rates between the two techniques.4-6 However, the recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia remains common owing to pulmonary-vein reconnection and extra–pulmonary-vein triggers.6,7 Thermal ab- lation also lacks tissue specificity, which increas- es the risk of rare complications involving adja- cent structures, including pulmonary-vein stenosis, phrenic-nerve palsy, and atrioesophageal fistula.8,9

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is a nonthermal ablation method with high preference for myo- cardial tissue.10 Preclinical11,12 and large observa- tional studies13 have confirmed its safety, with no reported case of atrioesophageal fistula to date. The ADVENT trial14 and nonrandomized studies15 have shown that PFA was associated with short- er procedural times than thermal ablation but with a similar incidence of recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia. However, these studies lacked continuous rhythm monitoring, which limited the sensitivity to detect the recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia and the burden of atrial fibril- lation. We conducted the SINGLE SHOT CHAMPION trial to compare PFA and cryoablation in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, with the recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia as- sessed with implantable cardiac monitors.

Methods

Trial Design and Oversight We conducted this investigator-initiated, random- ized, controlled, noninferiority trial at two tertiary care centers in Switzerland. The protocol (avail- able with the full text of this article at NEJM.org) was approved by the local ethics committees and followed Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was funded by Inselspital (University Hospital Bern) and University Hospital Basel, with an unrestricted research grant from Boston Scientific. The authors designed and conducted the trial and analyzed the data. The funders had

no role in the trial design or in the collection, monitoring, analysis, interpretation, or report- ing of the data. The first, fourth, and last authors wrote the first draft of the manuscript, made the decision to submit the manuscript for publica- tion, and vouch for the accuracy and complete- ness of the data and for the adherence of the trial to the protocol.

Patients We screened adults (≥18 years of age) who had symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, as documented on a 12-lead electrocardiogram or Holter monitor, lasting for at least 30 seconds in the past 24 months. All the patients were eligible for pulmonary-vein isolation according to cur- rent guidelines for the treatment of atrial fibril- lation. Patients were excluded from the trial if they had undergone previous left atrial ablation or surgery, received a diagnosis of atrial fibril- lation from reversible causes, or had severe mitral regurgitation, a left ventricular ejection fraction of less than 35%, or New York Heart Association class III or IV heart failure. Details regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Appendix, available at NEJM. org. All the patients provided written informed consent.

Randomization Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to undergo PFA or cryoablation. Central random- ization was performed with an interactive Web- based response system to ensure adequate con- cealment of assignment. The assignment sequence was computer-generated, with randomly permuted blocks and stratification according to trial center.

Interventions and Follow-up Patients underwent pulmonary-vein isolation with either a multielectrode pentaspline PFA cath- eter (Farapulse, Boston Scientific) or a cryobal- loon (Arctic Front, Medtronic), as detailed in the Supplementary Appendix. A repeat ablation pro- cedure within 3 months after the initial proce- dure (blanking period) was not permitted. Pro- cedures were performed under deep sedation with the use of midazolam, fentanyl, and propofol without the use of paralytics.16 Patients at high risk for sedation complications received general anesthesia.

All the patients received an implantable car- diac monitor (Reveal LinQ, Medtronic) for con- tinuous rhythm monitoring. The algorithm of the implantable cardiac monitor analyzed beat-to- beat variability to detect recurrence and burden of arrhythmia.17 The programmed measures of this device are summarized in the Supplementary Ap- pendix. The data from the implantable cardiac monitors were centrally uploaded to the core laboratory at Inselspital and adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee in a blind- ed manner. The recurrence of atrial tachyarrhyth- mia was defined as an episode of at least 120 sec- onds (the shortest-duration episode that could be programmed for detection on the implantable cardiac monitor) with a continuous atrial arrhyth- mia of at least 30 seconds. Antiarrhythmic drugs were permitted during the 3-month blanking period, but the discontinu- ation of such drugs was required thereafter. Fol- low-up included daily automated transmission of data from the implantable cardiac monitor and visits at 3, 6, and 12 months.

End Points The primary end point was the first recurrence of any atrial tachyarrhythmia (atrial fibrillation, flutter, or tachycardia) lasting at least 30 seconds during the period from day 91 to day 365 after the ablation procedure. The safety end point was a composite of car- diac tamponade leading to pericardiocentesis, persistent phrenic-nerve palsy lasting more than 24 hours, serious vascular complications lead- ing to intervention, stroke or transient ischemic attack, atrioesophageal fistula, or death within 30 days. The secondary arrhythmia end points were the first recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia during the period from day 1 to day 90 and the period from day 1 to day 365; the burden of atrial ar- rhythmia, which was defined as the percentage of time that the patient was in atrial arrhythmia dur- ing days 1 to 90 and days 91 to 365; and health- related quality of life, as assessed with the use of the EuroQol five-dimension, five-level (EQ-5D-5L) utility index (with scores ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health) at 3 and 12 months. The secondary clinical end points were death, stroke or transient ische­mic attack, unplanned hospitalization or emergency depart-

ment visit for the recurrence of atrial arrhythmia, cardioversion for recurrence of atrial arrhyth- mia, repeat ablation, and reinitiation of antiar- rhythmic drugs. All secondary clinical end points were assessed at 1 year. The burden of atrial ar- rhythmia during days 1 to 365, although not pre- specified, was reported for completeness. Pro- cedural end points included procedure time, left atrial indwelling time, fluoroscopy time, radia- tion dose, contrast-agent use, and high-sensitivity troponin levels on day 1 after ablation.

Statistical Analysis We hypothesized that PFA would be noninferior to cryoablation for the recurrence of atrial tachyar- rhythmia within 1 year after ablation, using a 90-day blanking period.18 The incidence of the recurrence, detected through implantable cardiac monitors, was assumed to be 40% at 1 year in both groups. We calculated that a sample size of 105 patients per group would provide the trial with more than 80% power to detect noninferi- ority at a margin of 20 percentage points for the difference in cumulative incidence,6 after account- ing for a 5% loss to follow-up. We calculated the difference in cumulative in- cidence using the Com-Nougue method.19 Non- inferiority was declared if the upper limit of the two-sided 95% confidence interval for the differ- ence in cumulative incidence (PFA minus cryo- ablation) remained below 20 percentage points in both the intention-to-treat and per-protocol populations, corresponding to a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 for noninferiority. The intention-to-treat population included all the patients who had undergone randomization and who were assessed according to their assigned treatment groups. The per-protocol population consisted of patients without major protocol de- viations. If noninferiority was met, superiority of PFA was tested in the intention-to-treat popula- tion, with superiority declared if the upper limit of the two-sided 95% confidence interval remained below 0, corresponding to a two-sided P value for superiority of less than 0.05. We analyzed the safety composite end point within 30 days using a risk ratio, procedural end points using general linear models, and the recur- rence of atrial tachyarrhythmia as a time-to-event outcome using differences in the cumulative in- cidence and Cox proportional-hazards models. Results

Patients From September 20, 2022, to November 16, 2023, a total of 210 patients were randomly assigned to undergo PFA (105 patients) or cryoablation (105 patients). Two patients who were assigned to the cryoablation group had premature explan- tation of the implantable cardiac monitors be- fore the occurrence of a primary-end-point event, and their data were censored at the time of their last monitor readouts at 214 and 239 days, respec- tively, after the ablation procedure (Fig. 1). The characteristics of the patients at baseline appeared to be well balanced between the two groups. The mean age of the patients was 64 years, and 28% were women (Table 1). The representativeness of the trial population is shown in Table S1 in the

Supplementary Appendix, and the site experience with PFA and cryoablation is shown in Table S2.

Treatment Characteristics All the patients underwent pulmonary-vein isola- tion as assigned. In 2 patients in the cryoablation group, an alternative cryoablation system (PolarX, Boston Scientific) was used owing to technical issues with the standard cryoablation system. The mean duration of the procedure was 55 minutes with PFA and 73 minutes with cryoablation (differ- ence, −18.3 minutes; 95% confidence interval [CI], −25.1 to −11.6); mean fluoroscopy times were 14.6 and 15.1 minutes, respectively. Cavotricuspid isthmus radiofrequency ablation was performed in 14 patients (13.3%) in the PFA group and 12 patients (11.4%) in the cryoablation group. No ablation outside the pulmonary veins was performed. The mean (±SD) high-sensitivity troponin levels in the PFA and cryoablation groups were 1920±954 ng per liter and 1114±419 ng per liter, respectively (difference, 823 ng per liter; 95% CI, 612 to 1034). A common ostium was detected in 7 patients (6.7%) and 21 patients (20.0%) in the PFA and cryoablation groups, respectively. Oth- er procedural characteristics and end points are reported in Tables S3, S4, and S5.

End Points A recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia between day 91 and day 365 (the primary end point) was observed in 39 patients in the PFA group and in 53 patients in the cryoablation group (Kaplan– Meier cumulative incidence, 37.1% and 50.7%, respectively; difference, −13.6 percentage points; 95% CI, −26.9 to −0.3; P<0.001 for noninferior- ity, P = 0.046 for superiority) (Fig. 2 and Table 2). In the per-protocol analysis of the primary end point, recurrence was observed in 39 of 105 pa- tients (37.1%) and 53 of 103 patients (51.7%) in the PFA and cryoablation groups, respectively (differ- ence, −14.6 percentage points; 95% CI, −28.0 to −1.2; P<0.001 for noninferiority). In the sensitivity analysis of the intention-to-treat population, which was adjusted for baseline covariates, the hazard ratio of the primary end point was 0.66 (95% CI, 0.43 to 1.01). a lower recurrence rate of atrial arrhythmia at 1 year among patients with symptomatic parox- ysmal atrial fibrillation. Recurrence rates were lower with PFA in both the intention-to-treat and per-protocol populations, with greater between- group differences observed during the blanking period (days 1 to 90). The burden of atrial ar- rhythmia was lower with PFA during the blanking period but similar during days 91 to 365. Safety outcomes were similar in the two groups, with few major adverse events in either group. Repeat ablations were numerically more frequent in the PFA group. The tissue-selective energy delivery and short application times of PFA offer potential safety and efficacy benefits over cryoablation. We ob- served no energy-related complications, such as pulmonary-vein stenosis, persistent phrenic-nerve palsy, or atrioesophageal fistula with PFA, find- ings that were consistent with those of large observational studies and the ADVENT trial.13,14 PFA procedures were shorter than cryoablation procedures, averaging less than 1 hour, which is less than half the time reported in previous ther- mal ablation trials.2,4,6,

A key feature of our trial was continuous rhythm monitoring by means of implantable car- diac monitors, a method that eliminated sampling

errors and enabled a more comprehensive assess- ment of ablation efficacy.20 A recurrence of atrial tachycardia was detected in approximately half the patients who were treated with cryo­ablation, a result that was consistent with the findings of previous trials that used continuous rhythm mon- itoring.2,6,21

Several factors may explain the reduction in the recurrence of atrial arrhythmia with PFA in our trial. First, PFA may create a broader ablation zone inside and around the pulmonary veins owing to the device design, which aligns with the concept of pulmonary-vein ablation rather than pulmonary- vein isolation. The larger troponin increase after PFA supports this hypothesis. Second, PFA may yield more durable lesions, thereby reducing pul- monary-vein reconnections. Although early stud- ies with protocol-mandated repeat procedures showed high lesion durability,22 subsequent real- world data were not as favorable.23,24

Continuous rhythm monitoring in our trial enabled a detailed assessment of treatment suc- cess. Early recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias is common after thermal ablation,25,26 often re- sulting from ablation-induced inflammation and autonomic imbalance, which justifies a blanking period. The results of our trial should be considered in the context of the findings of the ADVENT trial, in which 607 patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation were randomly assigned to undergo PFA or thermal ablation.14 The ADVENT trial showed no material between-group difference in the incidence of freedom from a composite end point of initial procedural failure, documented atrial tachyarrhythmia after a 3-month blanking period, antiarrhythmic drug use, cardioversion, or repeat ablation. Several factors may explain the

differing findings between our trial and the ADVENT trial. First, ADVENT operators had no previous clinical experience with PFA, which may have led to suboptimal outcomes. In contrast, all operators in our trial had at least 6 months of experience performing PFA (Table S2). Second, the ADVENT trial used intermittent Holter moni- toring rather than continuous rhythm monitor- ing, which resulted in lower recurrence detection and a reduced ability to detect between-group differences. Third, the ADVENT trial compared PFA with both cryoballoon and radiofrequency ablation, whereas our trial exclusively compared PFA with cryoablation. Our trial has several limitations. First, we used only the pentaspline PFA catheter, so our findings may not apply to other PFA systems. Second, im- plantable cardiac monitors were inserted after the ablation procedure, which prevented an assess- ment of the burden of atrial fibrillation at base- line. Third, a left common ostium was detected in 7% of the patients in the PFA group and in 20% of those in the cryoablation group. This random imbalance may have favored PFA, although a post hoc subgroup analysis according to the presence or absence of a left common ostium showed no difference in treatment effects between subgroups
